\textbf{Short answer: I couldn't show that it can, and I found a good reason to be cautious.}
The small model I tested already answers in Swedish, so there was nothing obvious to fix.
Asked to ``improve Swedish'' with no examples and no chance to test its changes, the editing
model changed nothing at all. When I deliberately broke a specific part of the network, it
found the broken part exactly --- but its repair didn't fix the behavior. My advice: don't
put engineering time into this yet. One cheap test would tell us whether it's worth
revisiting.

\section{What the original work shows}

The source report (Takehi, Teoh, Zhang and Khattab, MIT, November 2026) asks a frontier
model to read and write another network's weights the way a programmer reads code. Two
things in it are easy to conflate, and keeping them apart matters for us.

The first is \textbf{reasoning about weights with no feedback}: the model looks at numbers
and says what the network does, or writes a small network from scratch. The second is
\textbf{searching for an edit with feedback}: the model tries a change, runs the network,
sees the score, and tries again. Most of the headline gains come from the second. The
famous math result --- a small Qwen model going from 7.9\% to 48.1\% on a math benchmark
--- used fifty rounds of testing, and the edit removed a habit of looping until the answer
ran out of space. That is unblocking an ability the model already had, not teaching it a
new one. The authors tried the same approach on a smaller model that doesn't loop, and
couldn't improve it at all.

There is also a live disagreement about \emph{where} that math edit worked. A replication
with a different editor got similar gains purely by changing the output side of the network
--- scaling the final normalization and adjusting a couple of token preferences --- which is
close to changing sampling settings. The original authors point to evidence that their own
edit worked deeper in the network. Nobody has settled this.

So my reading is: reading weights well enough to locate structure looks real; adding a
capability a model lacks has not been shown; and the biggest improvement so far came from
repeated trial and error, not from understanding.

\section{The question I asked}

Swedish models are usually built by fine-tuning open models. Direct weight editing would
only be worth adopting if it beat the cheap things we already do --- writing a better
prompt, changing sampling settings, or a small fine-tune --- and if it changed something
real inside the network rather than just nudging which words come out.

I expected an edit to help most if the model had a specific, findable fault. That turned out
to be the whole story.

\section{What I did}

I used \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B}, the same model as the original report, on a laptop
processor with no graphics card. The editor was DeepSeek-V4-Pro.

The editor ran Python in a sandbox with read/write access to the model's raw weight tensors
--- it could inspect shapes, dtypes and slices, and compute its own statistics such as
per-row norms. It could not run the model or any part of it (both edit runs allowed no
forward passes), and it had no access to the original unbroken checkpoint, only a helper
that reported how many values had changed rather than their previous values. Because it
never saw the original weights, exact restoration of a planted fault was impossible in
principle, so a failed repair has to be judged against an approximate-repair bound rather
than exact recovery.

I measured three things: whether the model prefers grammatically correct Swedish sentences
(16 pairs, scored by likelihood, so sampling settings can't affect it); whether it actually
replies in Swedish (8 open questions); and whether its English factual knowledge survived
(10 questions). I kept the test items away from the editor.

I compared against an unchanged model, a Swedish instruction prefix, different sampling
settings, a bias toward Swedish words at output, a random edit of similar size, and a small
fine-tune on 20 Swedish question-answer pairs.

I perturbed 0.02\% of the weights for the random edit --- each by roughly one standard
deviation --- which serves as a ``does any sparse change move the grammar score'' check
rather than a magnitude match, since the ``improve Swedish'' edit ended up changing nothing
at all.

\textbf{There was nothing to fix.} The model already answered every Swedish question in
Swedish. The comparisons above can't separate from each other when the starting score is
full marks --- which is itself the finding.

\textbf{The editor changed nothing.} Given a vague goal, no examples and no way to test
anything, it spent around 116{,}000 tokens and left every weight untouched.

There is no stated reason to quote: the editor's final message was empty and hit the output
limit. The transcript is not ambiguous about this --- earlier turns show the loop running
code and receiving results --- so it was the editor exhausting its exploration budget
without ever writing a change, not a harness or parsing failure and not a deliberate
``nothing to fix'' abstention.

This is consistent with the original report's own limit: editing removes obstacles, it
doesn't add abilities.

\textbf{The fine-tune made things worse, which tells me my tests work.} After training on 20
examples, two answers came back in English and others degenerated into reciting the training
data. Asked who wrote Pippi Longstocking, it produced a multiple-choice list of
English-language authors. I treat this as a check that my measurements detect real damage,
not as a verdict on fine-tuning --- with 20 examples and untuned settings, this outcome is
unsurprising.

The fine-tune used a rank-8 LoRA (alpha 16, all linear layers, AdamW learning rate 2e-4) for
5 epochs over the 20 pairs at batch size 4 (25 steps). Config is in
\texttt{src/baselines/lora.py}; the training pairs are in
\texttt{src/baselines/swedish\_examples.py}.

\textbf{The interesting result: it found the break but couldn't fix it.} I multiplied 64
rows of one middle layer by six, which knocked word-order accuracy from 7/8 to 6/8. Given
the broken weights, the editor named the right tensor and edited exactly those 64 rows ---
229{,}376 values, nothing else. Word order stayed at 6/8. Precise localization, no repair.

The fault location and multiplier were chosen by convenience (the first rows of a middle
layer, scaled by a round factor), not by searching development or held-out items; I measured
the drop only after fixing the fault. Only the word-order fault (64 rows $\times$6) went
through the repair step --- the noun-agreement fault (48 rows $\times$4, agreement 7/8
$\rightarrow$6/8) was measured as a plant-only check and never repaired.

\section{How much to trust this}

These are small counts. A one-question swing moves a score by twelve points, and the grammar
drop above is not statistically significant on its own. What I lean on is the qualitative
pattern: an empty edit, an exact localization, and a repair that changed nothing measurable.

Other limits worth stating plainly. One model, one editor, one seed --- the original used a
different editor with more tools, so this doesn't refute their result. My language check is
a simple word-counting heuristic, fine for whole answers but blind to mixed-language text.
The break I planted was crude: a ten-line script flagging unusually large rows would find it
just as easily, so this test can't yet distinguish reasoning from arithmetic. And I never ran
the version with feedback, which is where the original's gains actually came from.

\section{Recommendation}

\textbf{Don't prioritise this for building stronger Swedish models.} Nothing I saw suggests
it adds ability a model lacks, and the model I tested had no language problem to solve. The
leverage for Swedish sits in data and evaluation, not in editing weights by hand.

\textbf{Treat the auditing claim as unproven rather than promising.} Finding an anomaly is
useful, but I haven't shown it beats a trivial statistical check, and the repair didn't work.

\textbf{The next experiment I'd run:} plant several faults of known location and subtlety,
then compare the frontier model against a short outlier-detection script on two separate
questions --- how often does each find the right place, and does either restore the
behavior? I'd include a copy-the-original-weights-back condition as an upper bound, and give
the model a small budget of test runs so I can see whether feedback is what makes the
difference. That is a day of work on a laptop, and it would tell us whether there's anything
here before we invest in it.

\section{Reproducing this}

Everything runs on CPU (no GPU required). Reproduce the main experiment with:

\begin{verbatim}
PYTHONPATH="$PWD/.pip_cpu2/site" python3 -u src/run_all.py \
  --model Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B \
  --arms base,prompt,decode,logit_bias,random_edit,lora,wsp_zero \
  --out results --cache-dir <HF_HOME>/hub --device cpu
\end{verbatim}

on \texttt{Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B} revision \texttt{2fc06364715b967f1860aea9cf38778875588b17},
torch 2.13.0+cu130, transformers 5.15.1, seed 0 (build the peft/pyyaml wheels first with
\texttt{scripts/setup\_cpu\_env.sh}). Editor transcripts and per-item results live under
\texttt{results/} (see \texttt{results/README.md}); AI tooling is described in
\texttt{ai\_usage.md}.
